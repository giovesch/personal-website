Baking sourdough bread is often described as an art, but in reality it is one of the purest domestic expressions of biochemical engineering. When you mix flour, water, salt, and wild yeast, you are orchestrating a living ecosystem of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* and heterofermentative *Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis*.

## The Baker's Percentage

For my benchmark country loaf:
- **Flour (Strong Manitoba / Type 1, 13.5% protein)**: 100% (500g)
- **Water (filtered, 26°C)**: 78% (390g)
- **Levain (mature, 100% hydration)**: 20% (100g)
- **Sea Salt**: 2.1% (10.5g)

## Gluten Network as a Viscoelastic Polymer

Gluten is formed when two primary storage proteins in wheat flour—**gliadin** and **glutenin**—hydrate and cross-link through intermolecular disulfide bonds ($-S-S-$).
- Gliadin provides viscous extensibility (allowing the dough to expand without tearing).
- Glutenin provides elastic tensile strength (allowing the dough to retain gas cells).

At 78% hydration, water molecules act as plasticizers, lowering dough viscosity. Rather than aggressive mechanical kneading, I rely on **autolysis** (a 45-minute rest period where endogenous proteases relax the dough) followed by periodic coil folds during bulk fermentation.

## Oven Spring and Steam Thermodynamics

When the cold dough (retarded at 4°C for 16 hours) hits a preheated cast iron Dutch oven at 245°C:
1. **Steam condensation**: Latent heat of vaporization is transferred directly to the dough surface, delaying gelatinization of starch and keeping the crust pliable.
2. **CO₂ expansion**: The ideal gas law ($PV = nRT$) dictates rapid volumetric expansion of trapped gas cells before the protein matrix sets.

The result is an open, honeycomb crumb with a crackling, blistered, caramelized crust.
